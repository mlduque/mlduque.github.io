How to warm up a new IP or domain

What IP and domain warm-up is, how long it takes, how to increase volume day by day and how to adapt it to Gmail, Microsoft and Yahoo without getting blocked.

What warm-up is and why it's essential

A new IP or domain has no history. To Gmail, Outlook or Yahoo, an unknown sender suddenly sending thousands of emails looks a lot like a spammer. Warm-up means starting with low volume and increasing it gradually so providers see that your emails get opened and don't cause complaints.

You need it when you start using a dedicated IP, when you switch sending platforms, when you start sending from a new domain and when you resume sending after several weeks of silence.

Before you start

  • Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC and check they're aligned.
  • Give the IP its own reverse DNS (PTR) pointing back to it. Check it in IP address information.
  • Sign up for Google Postmaster Tools and Microsoft SNDS to see how you're being received.
  • Prepare a list of your most active contacts: those who opened or clicked in the last 30 to 60 days. You'll start with them.

The plan: how much to increase each day

The general rule is to start with a few hundred emails a day and multiply volume by 1.3 to 2 each day, as long as results are good. Reaching tens of thousands of emails a day usually takes 3 to 6 weeks.

Work out your exact plan with the warm-up calculator: tell it the volume you want to reach and it gives you each day's sends, which you can download for Excel.

Always send at roughly the same time and spread volume through the day instead of releasing it all at once.

Each provider at its own pace

Not every provider accepts the same growth:

  • Microsoft (Outlook, Hotmail) is the strictest. Go slower, at most 25% a day, and watch for 4.7.650 bounces.
  • Gmail tolerates somewhat faster increases if complaints are low. Watch the spam rate in Postmaster Tools.
  • Yahoo and AOL slow you down with 421 4.7.0 [TSS04] deferrals when you go too fast.

The warm-up calculator lets you enter what share of your list uses each provider and gives you a separate plan for each one.

Signs to slow down

If you see any of these signs, repeat the previous day's volume instead of increasing, and if it gets worse, go down:

  • Temporary bounces (4xx) above 1% at a provider.
  • Complaints above 0.1%.
  • A clear drop in opens at a specific provider (it may mean you're landing in spam).
  • The IP shows up on a blocklist. Check it in My domain or IP address information.

Mistakes that ruin a warm-up

  • Starting by sending to your whole database, inactive contacts included.
  • Using old or purchased lists: that's exactly where spam traps are.
  • Pausing for several days halfway through and coming back at full volume.
  • Mixing marketing and transactional mail on the same IP from day one.
  • Sending unusual content (for example, a big promotion) right during the warm-up.

Checklist

  • ☐ SPF, DKIM, DMARC and PTR correct.
  • ☐ Postmaster Tools and SNDS set up.
  • ☐ I start with my most active contacts.
  • ☐ I have a day-by-day plan per provider.
  • ☐ I check bounces, complaints and opens every day before increasing.
  • ☐ I know when to slow down.

Fake opens and clicks: Apple, Gmail and bots

Why your open rates no longer tell the truth, who opens and clicks your emails without being a person, and what to measure instead.

By María Luisa Duque · Email Deliverability Manager · Updated 2026-11-17

In this guide
  1. Your statistics aren't what they seem
  2. Apple Mail: automatic opens
  3. Gmail: real but anonymous opens
  4. Antivirus clicks
  5. How to recognise a robot
  6. What to measure instead

Your statistics aren't what they seem

An open is measured with a tiny image (a tracking pixel) that downloads when the email is opened. For years it was a good sign of interest. Not anymore: some programs download that image without anyone opening the email, and antivirus filters click every link before the person sees it.

The result is inflated opens and clicks, «engaged» segments full of robots and campaigns that look like they perform better than they do.

Apple Mail: automatic opens

Since 2021, Apple Mail Privacy Protection (MPP) downloads the images of every email as soon as it arrives, through Apple's servers, even if the person never opens it. It affects anyone reading in the Mail app on iPhone, iPad or Mac with the option enabled, whatever their account: Gmail, Outlook or their company's.

You can spot them because they come from Apple IPs, with a minimal user agent (Mozilla/5.0) and very shortly after sending. In lists with lots of iPhones, more than half of opens can be automatic.

Gmail: real but anonymous opens

Gmail also downloads images through its own server (the «Google Image Proxy»), but only when the person opens the email. The open is real; what you lose is the device, the location and often repeat opens, because the image gets cached.

Yahoo does something similar with its own proxy. That's why these opens should count as human, even though they show the provider's IP.

Antivirus clicks

Corporate security filters (Microsoft Defender, Proofpoint, Mimecast, Barracuda…) open every link to check it doesn't lead to a dangerous site, usually before delivering the email or within the first seconds. On top of that come the previews WhatsApp, Slack or Teams generate when someone shares a link.

Besides inflating clicks, they can confirm double opt-in subscriptions or unsubscribe contacts without anyone asking. That's why Gmail and Yahoo's one-click unsubscribe works with a POST request (RFC 8058) rather than a simple link.

How to recognise a robot

  • User agent: scanner or program names (python, curl, HeadlessChrome) or very old browsers.
  • IP: Microsoft, Proofpoint, Mimecast, Barracuda or Apple ranges.
  • Speed: several links clicked within a few seconds, or clicks a few seconds after delivery.
  • Sweeps: the same IP clicking links for many different contacts in a minute.
  • Honeypot link: a link invisible to people; whoever clicks it is a robot.
  • Click without an open: suspicious, although it may be someone with images turned off.

What to measure instead

  • Use human clicks, purchases, replies and conversions as signs of interest, not opens.
  • To define «active» contacts, don't trust Apple opens: combine real clicks and activity on your website or store over the last 90 to 180 days.
  • Before removing inactive contacts, check they aren't customers using Apple Mail who never really «open».
  • Ask your platform to filter robot clicks and to let you export each event's user agent and IP.
  • Monitor reputation with Google Postmaster Tools and Microsoft SNDS, which measure what happens in the mailbox and don't rely on the pixel.

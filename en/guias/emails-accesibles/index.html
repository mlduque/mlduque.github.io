Accessible emails: how to make sure everyone can read your campaigns

What makes an email accessible, why it matters (including under the European Accessibility Act) and how to fix it step by step: language, alt text, contrast, font size, links and tables.

By María Luisa Duque · Email Deliverability Manager · Updated 2026-11-20

In this guide
  1. Why it matters
  2. Structure and code
  3. Images and alt text
  4. Text, colour and contrast
  5. Links and buttons
  6. How to check it
  7. Checklist

Why it matters

According to the World Health Organization, one in six people worldwide lives with a significant disability. Add to that people with colour blindness (around 8% of men), people who need reading glasses, people reading their phone in bright sunlight and people with images turned off. If your email isn't accessible, part of your list doesn't get the message, even when it reaches the inbox.

On top of that, the European Accessibility Act (Directive (EU) 2019/882) has applied since 28 June 2025. Among other things, it covers e-commerce aimed at consumers and refers to the EN 301 549 standard, which in turn is based on WCAG 2.1 level AA. Communications with your customers are part of the service, so the sensible approach is to apply those guidelines to your emails too. Micro-enterprises providing services (fewer than 10 people and up to €2 million in turnover) are exempt. This is general information, not legal advice.

The good news: almost everything that makes an email accessible also makes it easier for everyone to read and more trustworthy for spam filters.

Structure and code

  • Language: add lang to the <html> tag, for example <html lang="en">, so screen readers pronounce the text correctly.
  • Title: put the subject in <title>.
  • Layout tables: add role="presentation" to every table used only to position content. Otherwise screen readers announce "table with 3 rows and 2 columns" for each block.
  • Real headings: mark headings with <h1> and <h2> and style them inline. They let people jump from section to section.
  • Real text, not images: the main message, the price and the call to action should be text. An email that is a single image can't be read by anyone who doesn't load images.
  • Logical order: the order of the code is the reading order. On mobile, columns stack in that same order.

Images and alt text

  • Every image needs an alt attribute. Without it, screen readers read the file name ("banner underscore november dot jpg").
  • Images that say something: describe what matters, not the obvious. "Blue wool coat, 20% off" is better than "image of a coat".
  • Decorative images (dividers, ornaments, spacers): use an empty alt="" so they're skipped.
  • Images inside links: the alt should say where the link goes ("See the winter collection").
  • Text inside images: repeat it in the alt or, better, set it as text.
  • Animated GIFs: nothing that flashes more than three times a second, and the first frame should make sense on its own (desktop Outlook only shows that one).

Text, colour and contrast

  • Contrast: at least 4.5:1 between text and its background, and 3:1 for large text (24 px, or 19 px bold). Light grey on white is the most common mistake: #999999 on white only reaches 2.8:1.
  • Size: 16 px for body text and never below 12 px, not even in the footer.
  • Line height of 1.4 to 1.6, and short paragraphs.
  • Left alignment: avoid justified text and long centred paragraphs.
  • No sentences in capitals: they're harder to read and some screen readers spell them out. If you want the look, use text-transform:uppercase.
  • Don't rely on colour alone: "products in red are sold out" doesn't help someone who can't see red. Add a word or an icon.
  • Dark mode: check that logos and text are still readable when the client inverts the colours.

Links and buttons

  • Descriptive text: "See the winter catalogue" instead of "click here" or "read more". Many screen reader users browse the list of links without the surrounding text.
  • HTML buttons, not images: they can be read with images off and screen readers announce them as links.
  • Touch size: buttons at least 44 × 44 px with space between them, so people don't tap the wrong one.
  • Recognisable links: underlined or clearly different from body text, not by colour alone.
  • Avoid several links in a row with the same text that go to different places.

How to check it

  • My campaign: paste the HTML and you'll get an accessibility check with a score, the low-contrast text (with an alternative colour) and what to fix.
  • In the same tool, the advanced preview simulates colour blindness, blurred vision, images off and dark mode.
  • If you build the email with the newsletter builder, it already comes with lang, marked-up tables and alt text, and warns you if your brand colour has low contrast.
  • Try it with a real screen reader: VoiceOver on Mac and iPhone or NVDA (free) on Windows. Listen to your email from start to finish once: you'll learn more than from any tool.

Checklist

  • lang on <html> and the subject in <title>.
  • role="presentation" on every layout table.
  • Headings with <h1> and <h2>.
  • Main message and call to action in text, not in an image.
  • Descriptive alt on every image, and alt="" on decorative ones.
  • Minimum contrast of 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text).
  • Body text at 16 px; nothing below 12 px.
  • Left-aligned text, no sentences in capitals.
  • Links with descriptive text; HTML buttons at least 44 px tall.
  • Nothing that depends on colour alone.
  • Checked in dark mode and with images off.

How to get off Microsoft's block list (Outlook and Hotmail)

What Outlook's S3150, 5.7.511, 4.7.650 and 5.7.515 errors mean, how to find out why you're blocked and how to request delisting step by step.

By María Luisa Duque · Email Deliverability Manager · Updated 2026-11-17

  2. Identify your error

Why Microsoft is the hardest

Outlook.com, Hotmail, Live and MSN use their own filter and their own block list, with criteria Microsoft doesn't publish. Unlike Gmail, it doesn't show your reputation in a nice dashboard: you find out when your emails start bouncing or landing in the junk folder.

The good news: almost every Microsoft block can be fixed if you find the cause before requesting delisting. If you ask to be removed without fixing anything, you'll be back on the list within days.

Identify your error

Read the full bounce. Microsoft almost always includes a code in brackets that tells you what's going on:

  • 550 5.7.1 … (S3150) or (S3140): your IP is on Microsoft's block list. This is the most common case.
  • 550 5.7.511 Access denied, banned sender: a more serious block. The message itself asks you to forward the bounce to delist@microsoft.com.
  • 451 4.7.650: not a block but a temporary slowdown because of reputation. Microsoft accepts less of your mail until your reputation improves.
  • 550 5.7.515: your domain doesn't meet the authentication requirements for bulk senders (SPF, DKIM and DMARC). Requesting delisting won't fix it; configuring the domain will.

If the message makes no sense to you, paste it into the bounce translator to see what it means and what to do.

Before requesting delisting, fix the cause

Microsoft reviews your IP when you request removal. If it still sees the same problem, it will say it can't remove you. Check these first:

  • Authentication: SPF, DKIM and DMARC set up correctly and aligned with the sender's domain. Check it with Gmail, Yahoo and Microsoft requirements.
  • Reverse DNS (PTR) for the IP with its own name, not a generic one, pointing back to the same IP. Check it in IP address information.
  • List quality: remove bouncing addresses, contacts inactive for more than 6 to 12 months and anyone who didn't give you permission. Microsoft's spam traps hide in old or purchased lists.
  • Complaints: if many users mark your emails as spam, Microsoft blocks you even if everything technical is fine.
  • Volume: a sudden spike from a new or cold IP is one of the most common causes.

Sign up for SNDS and JMRP

Microsoft offers two free tools worth having before you ask for anything:

  • SNDS (Smart Network Data Services): shows per IP how much email Microsoft accepts, the complaint rate and whether any IP is hitting spam traps. The green, yellow or red status is the best clue to what's happening.
  • JMRP (Junk Mail Reporting Program): sends you a copy of every email an Outlook user marks as spam, so you can unsubscribe complainers and see which campaigns cause complaints.

JMRP reports arrive in ARF format. Paste them into the complaint analyser to see who complained and about which campaign.

Request delisting step by step

  1. Open the Outlook.com sender support form (Sender Support, at olcsupport.office.com). If the bounce comes from a company that uses Microsoft 365, the portal is sender.office.com.
  2. Enter the blocked IP (or range), your contact email and paste the full bounce with its code.
  3. Explain in a few lines what you've fixed, for example: «we've cleaned the list, set up DKIM and reduced volume».
  4. Reply to the automatic email you'll receive: it usually asks you to confirm the request.
  5. Wait for the answer, usually within 24 to 48 hours. It may say you've been fully removed, removed «conditionally» (you're monitored for a few days) or that you don't qualify.

If they say you don't qualify, reply in the same thread asking them to escalate and explain again what you've changed. Data helps (SNDS green, low complaints).

After you're out: don't get back in

  • Increase volume to Microsoft gradually, as in a warm-up: Microsoft is the provider most sensitive to sudden changes.
  • Send to your most active Outlook and Hotmail contacts first.
  • Keep the complaint rate below 0.1%.
  • Check SNDS every week and watch for 4.7.650 bounces: they're the first warning.
  • Turn on the Telegram alerts to find out if your domain lands on a blocklist.

Checklist

  • ☐ I've read the bounce and know my code (S3150, 5.7.511, 4.7.650 or 5.7.515).
  • ☐ SPF, DKIM and DMARC are correct and aligned.
  • ☐ The IP has its own PTR pointing back to it.
  • ☐ I've cleaned the list and removed inactive contacts.
  • ☐ I'm signed up for SNDS and JMRP.
  • ☐ I've requested delisting and explained what I fixed.
  • ☐ I'll ramp up volume to Microsoft gradually.

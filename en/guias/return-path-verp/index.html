Return-Path and VERP: where your bounces go

Every email's hidden sender: what the Return-Path is for, how it affects SPF and DMARC, and how VERP tells you exactly which address bounced.

By María Luisa Duque · Email Deliverability Manager · Updated 2026-11-17

In this guide

  2. What the Return-Path is for
  3. Return-Path and DMARC: alignment

Every email has two senders

Just as a letter has the sender's address on the envelope and a signature inside, an email has two senders. The one you see is the From (the «From» header). The other is the envelope sender, which the server states with the MAIL FROM command when it connects. When the email is delivered, the receiving server stores it in the Return-Path header.

Hardly anyone sees it, but it decides two important things: where bounces go and which domain SPF checks.

What the Return-Path is for

  • Bounces: if an email can't be delivered, the error notice (the bounce or DSN) goes to the Return-Path, never to the From. That way your customer service inbox doesn't fill up with errors.
  • SPF: SPF doesn't check the From domain but the Return-Path domain. If your platform uses its own bounce domain, SPF passes… with the platform's domain.

You can see it in any email: in Gmail, «Show original» and look for the Return-Path: line.

Return-Path and DMARC: alignment

DMARC requires SPF or DKIM to pass with the same domain as the From. If the Return-Path is bounces.platform.com and your From is yourcompany.com, SPF passes but isn't aligned, and everything rests on DKIM. If the DKIM signature ever fails, for example on a forward, DMARC fails.

The solution is your own bounce domain: a subdomain such as bounces.yourcompany.com that the platform asks you to point at its servers with a CNAME or MX record. That way SPF aligns too and you have two ways to pass DMARC.

What VERP is

Bounces are chaos: every server writes them its own way and many don't clearly say which address failed, especially if the contact forwarded their mail to another account. VERP (Variable Envelope Return Path) solves this by using a different Return-Path for each recipient.

Instead of bounces@yourcompany.com, each email goes out with something like b-7f3a91-maria=example.com@bounces.yourcompany.com. When the bounce arrives, the address it reaches already tells you which contact (and which campaign) failed, without having to read the error message. The idea comes from Dan Bernstein, the author of qmail, in 1997, and today every sending platform uses it.

How to set it up properly

  • Use a dedicated subdomain for bounces, with its own MX record pointing to whoever processes bounces and its own SPF.
  • Encode an identifier (not the full address in clear text, if you can) and sign it with a small check code: that way you discard fake bounces and stop others using you to send backscatter.
  • Respect the 64-character limit of the address's local part.
  • Also process asynchronous bounces (those arriving hours later) and complaint notices (feedback loops).
  • Remove hard bounces immediately and retry soft ones sensibly.

How to check it

Send yourself an email from your platform, open «Show original» and look at the Return-Path: it should be on your domain and change with every send if you use VERP. Then look up the MX record and SPF of that bounce subdomain with the Dig tool. And when you get a bounce, paste it into the bounce translator to find out what it means.
